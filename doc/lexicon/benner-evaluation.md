# Jeff Benner's letter-meaning approach: an evaluation

Jeff A. Benner, founder of the Ancient Hebrew Research Center
(ancient-hebrew.org), has done more than almost anyone to interest ordinary
readers in the Hebrew behind their Bibles. His *Ancient Hebrew Lexicon of the
Bible* (2005) and his many articles ask readers to hear the text as its first
hearers did, in concrete terms. This page weighs his central method, reading
meaning from the pictures behind the letters, against the evidence. It says
what the Haqor lexicon takes from his work and what it does not.

It builds on [the origin of the Hebrew alphabet](alphabet-origin.md); the
method the lexicon follows instead is in
[building an entry](building-an-entry.md).

## The approach

Benner's method has four parts:

1. **Each letter has a meaning** from its original picture and what that
   object meant in a nomadic, pastoral culture. In his chart א is an ox head
   (strength, leader), ב a tent's floor plan (house, family, inside), ע an eye
   (watch, know), and ש two front teeth (sharp, press, two).
2. **Two-letter "parent roots"** combine their letters' meanings. His
   best-known example is אב "father", which he reads as the ox's strength and
   the tent: the strength of the house.
3. **Three-letter "child roots"** grow from a parent root by adding a letter,
   and share its meaning. Roots that fit no family are "adopted".
4. **Hebrew thought is concrete**, unlike the abstract thought of Greek, so a
   word's original sense is something that can be seen, touched or done.

## What it gets right

- **Concrete meaning.** Many Hebrew words do have a concrete core that later
  usage extends: רוּחַ is wind and breath before it is spirit; כָּבוֹד is
  weight before it is glory. Looking for that core is a sound instinct, and
  the Haqor lexicon looks for it too, in the verses.
- **Roots form families.** Words built on one root belong together, and
  Benner teaches readers to see them together. The lexicon keeps all of a
  root's words in one entry for the same reason.
- **Biliteral bases are a serious question.** Scholars have long argued that
  many three-letter roots grew from two-consonant bases: פרד, פרץ, פרק, פרר
  and פרש all have to do with separating or breaking. Benner's parent roots
  take that question seriously.
- **The letters' names do keep their pictures.** The names *aleph*, *bet*,
  *ayin* do mean ox, house and eye, and the earliest letter shapes were those
  pictures. Benner is right about where the letters came from.
- **He brings readers to the text.** His work starts many people reading
  Hebrew for themselves, which is a real service.

## Where the evidence does not support it

### The pictures stand for sounds

The alphabet was made by *acrophony*: each picture stands for the first sound
of the Semitic word for the thing pictured (ox, *ʾalp*, for ʾ). The ox head
was chosen for א because the word for ox began with that sound, not because
the sound meant strength. This holds both in the mainstream account and in
Douglas Petrovich's (see [the alphabet's origin](alphabet-origin.md)). On
either account the picture is a memory aid for a sound.

### The language came before the letters

Semitic languages were spoken, with their roots already formed, long before
the alphabet. Akkadian shares roots with Hebrew (*šamû*, שָׁמַיִם) and was
written in cuneiform centuries before the first alphabetic inscription. A
script invented later cannot have shaped the meanings of roots that already
existed. The direction runs from the words to the pictures.

### The pictures were not fixed

The earliest alphabetic inscriptions show more than one form for some
letters, and later writing settled on one. If a letter's meaning came from
its picture, it would have changed when the picture did. Benner's chart
reflects the forms that became standard, which were a convention that
emerged, not one fixed from the start.

### One letter covers several older sounds

The 22 letters cover about 29 older consonants. So in some roots ע stands for
ġ, a different sound from the ʿ of *ʿayn* "eye":

- עֶרֶב "evening" has ġ (Arabic *ġarb* "sunset, west");
- עַזָּה Gaza has ġ (Septuagint Γάζα);
- עֹרֵב "raven" has ġ (Arabic *ġurāb*).

In the same way ש stands for ṯ in שָׁלֹשׁ "three" (Aramaic תְּלָת, Arabic
*ṯalāṯ*) and in אשר (Aramaic אֲתַר "place", Arabic *ʾaṯar* "footprint").
When these words were coined, those sounds were not written with the eye or
the teeth at all. A letter's picture cannot be part of the meaning of a root
whose sound it took over only when two sounds merged.

### Aramaic writes the same roots with other letters

Hebrew זָהָב and Aramaic דְּהַב (Daniel 2:32) are one word for gold, from one
older ḏ. Hebrew writes it with ז (in Benner's chart a cutting tool) and Aramaic with
ד (the tent door). If the letters' pictures made up its meaning, the same word
would mean different things in the two halves of Daniel. Old Aramaic wrote it
first with ז and changed to ד as its pronunciation moved: the letter followed
the sound.

### Related languages share roots in unrelated scripts

Arabic, Ge'ez, Akkadian and Ugaritic share roots and meanings with Hebrew,
written in scripts whose signs have nothing to do with the Hebrew pictures.
The Greeks took the very same letters and names (*alpha, beta*) without any
of their meanings. Meaning travels with sounds, whatever script records them.

### The readings are hard to test

Each letter carries several extended meanings (in his chart ע is eye, watch, know,
shade). Combining two letters' meanings can fit many possible senses, and
the reading is made once the word's meaning is already known. A method that
can fit almost any outcome after the fact cannot show that the fit is more
than chance. A testable form would predict a root's meaning from its letters
before its use is looked at, or show that roots containing a letter share its
meaning more often than roots that do not. The method has not been tested
in either way.

### Root meaning and use

The approach treats the root's meaning (or the letters') as what a word
really means. James Barr, in *The Semantics of Biblical Language* (1961),
showed why this is unsafe: a word means what its users use it to mean, which
can move a long way from its root. He made the same case against arguing
from language structure to a "Hebrew" way of thinking opposed to a "Greek"
one. Hebrew has abstract words too (אֱמוּנָה faithfulness, צְדָקָה
righteousness), and scripture's own use defines them.

## What the Haqor lexicon takes from his work

- **The search for the concrete core**, carried out in the verses: the senses
  that the text's own use fixes.
- **Roots as families**, one entry per spelling with all its words.
- **Parent-root families as a question to test, not a result.** Families
  should be grouped by the older sounds cognates show, not by Hebrew letters
  that hide merged sounds; `db lexicon-splits` and the `sounds` the entries
  record are the start of that.
- **His lexicon as a lead**, like any other: where it gives a sense, follow it
  to the verses, and cite those.

What the lexicon does not take is **letter meanings as evidence**. No entry
explains a word from the pictures of its letters.

## A test the project could run

The claim that letters carry meaning makes a prediction that can be checked
with the project's data. If ע meant "eye, see, know", roots containing ע
should have glosses about seeing or knowing more often than roots without it,
and more often than roots whose ע cognates show was once ġ. The same can be
checked for each letter. If the data showed such a pattern, it would deserve
serious attention. If not, that would settle the question for this lexicon
from evidence, not assertion.
