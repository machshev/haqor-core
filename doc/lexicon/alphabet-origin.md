# The origin of the Hebrew alphabet, and why it matters to the lexicon

The lexicon explains a word from scripture's use of it and from its cognates,
not from the shapes of its letters. This page sets out why: how the alphabet
came to be, what its letters stood for, and what that means for reading
meaning out of a root. The companion page,
[an evaluation of Jeff Benner's approach](benner-evaluation.md), applies it to
the best-known attempt to read meaning out of the letters. The steps for
writing an entry are in [building an entry](building-an-entry.md).

## The spoken language came first

Semitic languages were spoken long before anyone wrote them in an alphabet.
Akkadian was written in cuneiform, a script of syllables and word signs, from
the middle of the third millennium BC, and its words already share their roots
with Hebrew (Akkadian *šamû* "heaven", Hebrew שָׁמַיִם). The root system, with
its three-consonant roots and its patterns of vowels and affixes, is shared by
languages that never used the alphabet. It belongs to the spoken language, and
any script was fitted to it afterwards.

## How the alphabet was invented: acrophony

The earliest alphabetic inscriptions are the Proto-Sinaitic texts from the
Egyptian turquoise mines at Serabit el-Khadim in Sinai (found in 1904–05), and
the Wadi el-Hol inscriptions in Egypt (found in the 1990s), dated to the
Middle Kingdom, around the 19th–18th centuries BC. Their writers were Semitic
speakers who knew Egyptian hieroglyphs.

They took a small set of pictures, many modelled on hieroglyphs, and gave each
the **first sound of the Semitic word for the thing pictured**. This is
*acrophony*:

| Picture | Word | Letter | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| ox head | *ʾalp* | א | ʾ |
| house | *bayt* | ב | b |
| water | *mayim* | מ | m |
| eye | *ʿayn* | ע | ʿ |
| hand | *yad* | י | y |
| head | *raʾš* | ר | r |

Alan Gardiner showed this in 1916 by reading a repeated Sinai group as
*lbʿlt* "to Baʿalat", the goddess of the mines. Acrophony explains why the
script writes consonants only (Semitic words begin with a consonant), and why
the letters' names keep their old meanings after the shapes stopped looking
like pictures.

## Douglas Petrovich: the alphabet was Hebrew

Douglas Petrovich, in *The World's Oldest Alphabet: Hebrew as the Language of
the Proto-Consonantal Script* (Carta, 2016), argues that:

- the earliest alphabetic script was invented by Hebrews in Egypt, in the
  Middle Kingdom period he assigns to Joseph and Israel's stay in Egypt;
- its language is Hebrew in particular: he reads sixteen inscriptions, from
  about 1842 to 1446 BC, as Hebrew;
- each letter can be matched to a particular Egyptian hieroglyph, its sound
  taken from the first consonant of the Hebrew word for the thing pictured;
- some inscriptions name people known from scripture: he reads the names
  Asenath, Ahisamach and Moses.

The project takes this as its working account. The case rests on the
inscriptions themselves, and the evidence is weighed below, not counted by
who holds which view.

### His dates and inscriptions

Petrovich gives these as the earliest Hebrew inscriptions, all from the reign
of Amenemhat III (his reply to Christopher Rollston of 20 December 2016):

| Date | Inscription | Where |

| 1840 BC | Sinai 377 (year 20 of Amenemhat III), "the oldest fully Hebrew inscription" | Wadi Nasb, Sinai |

| 1831 BC | the Lahun bilingual ostracon, found by Petrie | Lahun, Middle Egypt |

The latest he reads is Sinai 361, about 1446 BC: "Our bond servitude had
lingered. Moses then provoked astonishment. It is a year of astonishment."

### His letters

Petrovich counts **22 letters from the start, not 27**. Earlier charts
(Albright's among them) gave the early script 27 signs, some for sounds Hebrew
writes with one letter. Petrovich takes the extra signs as forms of the 22,
and gives each letter a Middle Egyptian hieroglyph as its exemplar and a
Hebrew word as its acrophone. His chart, *Hebrew Consonantals* (© 2015), is
[published by the Ancient Hebrew Research Center](https://www.ancient-hebrew.org/alphabet/files/hebrew-alphabet-evolution-petrovich-chart.pdf).
It also draws each letter as written in the Middle Kingdom (about 1842–1760
BC), the New Kingdom (about 1560–1307 BC) and Iron Age Canaan (about
1150–587 BC), with the inscriptions each form comes from.

The exemplars, sign numbers, acrophones and NIVEC numbers are Petrovich's;
the short descriptions of the Egyptian signs are added here. His chart draws
no Middle Kingdom form for ט, צ or ש: their earliest forms are from the New
Kingdom.

Two things in the chart bear on the lexicon:

- **ח and ס each have two exemplars, and the two acrophones begin with two
  different older sounds.** חָצֵר "enclosure" has ḥ, while חוּט "thread" has
  ḫ (Arabic خيط *ḫayṭ*). שֵׂעָר "hair" has ś, while סָרַח has s. The chart
  does not say so, but the first writers appear to have had a sign for each
  sound of a pair that the 22 letters later write with one letter. This is
  the same merger the lexicon's `sounds` record (see the table under
  "One letter, several older sounds").
- **Some acrophones come from what the Egyptian sign meant, not from what it
  pictured.** F35 is the Egyptian *nfr* "good", and ט is טוֹב "good". The
  letter is still made by acrophony, from the first sound of a Hebrew word.

The letters lost their pictures over the centuries: Petrovich traces this as
their "residual pictographic value", less in each period from the Middle
Bronze Age to the Iron Age.

### Distinctively Hebrew words

Petrovich answers the charge that the inscriptions' words are common Semitic
by reading words that he argues only Hebrew has. In Sinai 349: "They yearned
for Hathor, but the quiver of our brothers was thoroughly despised", where
תְּלִי "quiver" (Genesis 27:3) and אַשְׁפָּה "quiver" stand for the Hebrews'
children, and the oppressor "performed terror (אֵימָה) against their quiver"
(compare Psalm 127:4–5, "as arrows… happy is the man that hath his quiver
full of them"). Another line of Sinai 349: "He sought occasion to cut away to
barrenness our great number, our swelling without measure", as Pharaoh did in
Exodus 1. He holds that a corpus with several such words, and with names
found only in the Hebrew Bible, is Hebrew as a whole, as any Iron Age Hebrew
inscription is.

### Weighing the evidence

What the inscriptions show on any reading is firm: Semitic speakers living in
Egypt and working in Sinai made the alphabet in the Middle Kingdom, from
Egyptian hieroglyphs, for their own language. Egypt's own records put large
numbers of such people there at that time. Excavations at Tell el-Dabʿa
(Avaris), in the eastern Delta where Genesis places Goshen, found a large
Semitic settlement growing from the late 12th dynasty. Egyptian expedition
inscriptions at Serabit el-Khadim name Semitic men from Retjenu (Canaan and
Syria) among the mining parties.

The step from "Semitic" to "Hebrew" rests on particular readings: a word, a
name, a grammatical form that Hebrew has and its neighbours lack. The texts are
short, written in consonants only, and some are worn, so a reading can turn
on one doubtful letter. The dialects of Canaan in the second millennium also
differed less than their later forms do. That is where Petrovich's case should
be checked, inscription by inscription.

Scripture is evidence too. It puts Israel in Egypt in this period, multiplying
in Goshen and kept at forced labour (Exodus 1). If Semitic speakers in Egypt
at that time made a script for their language, scripture names the people who
were there. The individual readings may be refined. The overall picture is
of a Semitic people in Egypt writing their own tongue, and scripture says who
that people was.

One point turns on scripture itself: how long Israel was in Egypt. Both
readings have scriptural evidence.

For 430 years in Egypt:

- Exodus 12:40–41 (Masoretic text): "the sojourning of the children of
  Israel, who dwelt in Egypt, was four hundred and thirty years".
- Genesis 15:13 (quoted in Acts 7:6): Abram's seed will be strangers in a
  land not theirs, serving and afflicted, four hundred years.
- Israel grows from seventy souls (Genesis 46:27) to 603,550 men of fighting
  age (Numbers 1:46).
- 1 Chronicles 7:20–27 counts about ten generations from Ephraim to Joshua.
- At Sinai the Amramites are one of four Kohathite clans, with 8,600 Kohathite
  males (Numbers 3:27–28): too many to be the descendants of Moses' own father.
  That suggests the Amram of Exodus 6:20 is a forebear several generations
  back, and the genealogy is shortened, as genealogies often are.

For 215 years in Egypt:

- Galatians 3:17: the law came 430 years after the covenant with Abraham.
  From the promise (Abram aged 75, Genesis 12:4) to Jacob's arrival in Egypt
  is 25 + 60 + 130 = 215 years (Genesis 21:5, 25:26, 47:9), which leaves 215
  in Egypt. The long reading answers that the promise was confirmed last to
  Jacob as he went down (Genesis 46:3–4), and the count can start there.
- The Septuagint and the Samaritan Pentateuch at Exodus 12:40 add "and in the
  land of Canaan".
- Genesis 15:16: "in the fourth generation they shall come hither again":
  Levi, Kohath, Amram, Moses (Exodus 6:16–20).
- Jochebed, Moses' mother, was "the daughter of Levi, whom her mother bare to
  Levi in Egypt" (Numbers 26:59). If Levi's own daughter bore Moses, the stay
  cannot have been 430 years. On the long reading "daughter" means descendant.
- Kohath came down with Jacob (Genesis 46:11). His years (133), Amram's (137)
  and Moses' 80 at the exodus add up to at most 350 years.

With the exodus about 1446 BC, the long stay puts Jacob's arrival about
1876 BC and the short one about 1661 BC. On the conventional Egyptian dates
Petrovich uses, the earliest inscriptions (about 1842 BC) fall in Joseph's
lifetime only on the long stay. The inscriptions' dates depend on the Egyptian
chronology as much as on scripture's (see David Rohl's chronology, below).

### Petrovich and David Rohl

David Rohl (*A Test of Time*, 1995) also places Joseph and Israel in the
Middle Kingdom, and the exodus about 1447 BC. But he lowers Egypt's dates by
up to about three and a half centuries (his "New Chronology"), which Petrovich does not accept.

| | Petrovich | Rohl |
|---|---|---|
| Exodus | about 1446 BC (1 Kings 6:1) | about 1447 BC |
| Egyptian dates | conventional | lowered, by up to about 350 years |
| Stay in Egypt | 430 years | 215 years |
| Joseph | 12th dynasty | 12th dynasty (Amenemhat III) |
| Pharaoh of the exodus | Amenhotep II (18th dynasty) | Dudimose (13th dynasty) |
| Israel's settlement | Avaris, Middle Kingdom | Avaris; the tomb with a Semitic official's statue read as Joseph's |

They reach the same biblical dates by different routes, because the 12th
dynasty sits about 200 years later on Rohl's dates. If those dates were right,
the earliest alphabetic inscriptions would fall after 1661 BC, during the
short stay, and Petrovich's reading of them as Hebrew would fit the short
stay too. Both put the first inscriptions under Amenemhat III, whom Rohl makes
Joseph's pharaoh.

On the inscriptions, Rohl works with Michael Shelomo Bar-Ron, who also reads
them as Hebrew (the film *The Moses Controversy*, and Patterns of Evidence's
articles). They and Petrovich agree on the language and on Israel's presence,
but differ on particular readings: Bar-Ron finds Moses in Sinai 357 ("This is
from Moses"), Petrovich in Sinai 361. Rohl asks whether Joseph made the
alphabet and Moses wrote some of the inscriptions, without, in what was found
for this page, settling either. Rohl writes (*Legendary Kings*, 2021, as
adapted in "The Golden Calf") that the Serabit inscriptions show the
Semites working the mines before the exodus worshipped Hathor as Baʿalat, and
he ties the calf at Sinai to that worship. Neither has been found commenting
on the other's work.

### What it means for scripture

**Moses could write, and so could Israel.** Scripture has Moses writing from
the start of the wilderness: "Write this for a memorial in a book"
(Exodus 17:14), the words of the covenant (Exodus 24:4, 34:27), the stages of
the journey (Numbers 33:2), the whole law (Deuteronomy 31:9, 24). The old
claim that writing came too late for Moses is answered by the inscriptions on
any reading. On Petrovich's, the script was Israel's own, centuries old when
Moses wrote.

**Writing for everyone, not only for scribes.** Hieroglyphs took years of
scribal training. An alphabet of a few dozen signs can be learnt in days, and
its inventors were miners and labourers, not scribes. The law expects that
kind of literacy. Every household writes the words on its doorposts
(Deuteronomy 6:9, 11:20). A man writes his own bill of divorce
(Deuteronomy 24:1). The king copies the law himself (Deuteronomy 17:18). A
young man of Succoth writes down the names of its elders (Judges 8:14). The
alphabet made a people able to keep a written word.

**Israel kept records in Egypt.** In Exodus 5:6–19 the שֹׁטְרִים, the
"officers of the children of Israel" whom Pharaoh's taskmasters set over them,
keep the tally of bricks and answer for it. The root שטר is Akkadian *šaṭāru*
"to write", and Aramaic שְׁטָר is a written deed: the officers were Israel's
own record-keepers.

**Written records from the patriarchs.** Genesis 5:1 calls its genealogy "the
book of the generations of Adam". If Hebrews were writing in Joseph's day,
records reaching Moses in writing are possible. Scripture does not say how
Moses gathered Genesis, and the inscriptions do not show it, but they remove
the objection that no one could have written it down.

**Life in Egypt as scripture tells it.** The Sinai inscriptions are prayers
and dedications at the mines, many to *Baʿalat*, "the Lady", the Semitic name
for Hathor, the goddess of the temple at Serabit el-Khadim. Hathor was shown
as a cow. Scripture says Israel served other gods in Egypt: "put away the gods
which your fathers served on the other side of the flood, and in Egypt"
(Joshua 24:14; Ezekiel 20:7–8, 23:3). The calf at Sinai (Exodus 32) was made
in the same wilderness, by a people who had known that worship, as David
Rohl also argues (above). Petrovich also reads inscriptions speaking of
bondage and of the oppressor cutting down their numbers, as Exodus 1 does.

**El before יַהְוֶה's name was known.** The inscriptions name God as *ʾl*,
El. Exodus 6:3: "I appeared unto Abraham, unto Isaac, and unto Jacob, by the
name of El Shaddai, but by my name יַהְוֶה was I not known to them."

**Names from Exodus and Genesis.** Petrovich's readings of Asenath, Joseph's
wife (Genesis 41:45), and Ahisamach, father of Oholiab the engraver (Exodus
31:6, 38:23), are the most direct links. Oholiab's work was engraving
(Exodus 35:35). The names given to Hebrews in Egypt also appear in Egyptian
lists: a 13th-dynasty household list (Brooklyn Papyrus 35.1446) names many
Semitic servants, one read as Shiphrah, the name of a Hebrew midwife in
Exodus 1:15.

### What it means for the lexicon

The three points the lexicon rests on hold on Petrovich's account:

1. The letters were made by **acrophony**: each picture stands for the first
   sound of a word.
2. The script was **fitted to a language already spoken**, with its roots
   already formed.
3. The earliest inscriptions show **several forms for some letters**, which
   later writing settled into one. Petrovich traces the pictures
   simplifying over the centuries.

Petrovich adds three more:

- **The 22 letters were chosen for Hebrew.** Petrovich counts 22 letters from
  the first inscriptions. If Hebrews made the alphabet with 22,
  they wrote two spoken sounds with one letter from the start: Γάζα shows ġ
  still spoken under ע more than a thousand years later. Twenty-two letters
  were enough to record the language without spelling every sound apart. The
  sound splits the lexicon records are differences in speech that the
  script, by design, did not mark.
- **Hebrew words from Egypt.** The Hebrew of Genesis and Exodus has words
  from Egyptian where the story is set in Egypt: פַּרְעֹה (*pr-ʿꜣ* "great
  house"), יְאֹר "the Nile" (*itrw*), סוּף "reeds" (*ṯwf*), גֹּמֶא
  "papyrus" (*qmꜣ*), תֵּבָה "chest" (*ṯbt*), אָחוּ "reed grass" (*ꜣḫ*),
  חַרְטֹם "magician" (*ḥry-tp*). An entry for such a word should record its
  Egyptian source as a loan (`egy`).
- **The inscriptions are Hebrew evidence outside scripture.** The words read
  in them are the earliest written Hebrew. They rank below scripture's use
  and, being Hebrew itself, above cognates.

## What the letters carried: sounds, not meanings

If each letter stands for a sound because of a word's first consonant, the
picture is a memory aid for that sound. The words of the language were not
built from the pictures; the pictures were chosen to fit sounds the words
already had. Five lines of evidence agree.

### One letter, several older sounds

Comparing the Semitic languages shows that their common ancestor had about 29
consonants. The 22 letters write fewer sounds than were spoken (some had
merged in speech, others were still spoken apart but written with one letter),
so five Hebrew letters each cover more than one older sound:

| Letter | Older sounds | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ח | ḥ, ḫ | Septuagint Ισαακ (יִצְחָק, with ḥ: Arabic *ḍaḥika* "laugh"), written with no consonant, beside Χαρραν (חָרָן, with ḫ: Akkadian *Ḫarrānu*), written χ |
| ע | ʿ, ġ | עַזָּה is Γάζα, עֲמֹרָה is Γόμορρα: the ġ was still spoken when the Septuagint was made |
| ש | š, ṯ | שָׁלֹשׁ "three", Aramaic תְּלָת, Arabic ثلاث *ṯalāṯ* |
| ז | z, ḏ | זָהָב "gold", Aramaic דְּהַב (Daniel 2:32), Arabic ذهب *ḏahab* |
| צ | ṣ, ṣ́, ṯ̣ | אֶרֶץ "land", Aramaic אַרְעָא; צֵל "shade", Aramaic טְלַל |

Other Semitic alphabets kept more of these sounds apart: Ugaritic's cuneiform
alphabet (13th century BC) had 30 signs, and Old South Arabian 29 letters. A
letter of the 22 does not stand for one thing. It stands for whatever sounds
had fallen together in the dialect the alphabet was made for.

### Aramaic spelled the same words with different letters

Early Aramaic scribes borrowed the 22 letters for a language that still kept
some of the older sounds apart, and wrote each with the nearest letter:
Old Aramaic writes the particle "which" (later דִּי) as זי, and "land" as ארק.
When their pronunciation moved, they changed the spelling: ḏ came to be
written ד, and ṣ́ came to be written ע. Jeremiah 10:11, in Aramaic, has
both spellings of "earth" in one verse: אַרְקָא and אַרְעָא. The letters
followed the pronunciation, never the other way round.

### Scripture records accent

Judges 12:6 records the Ephraimites saying *sibboleth* for *shibboleth*. The
men of Gilead heard the difference, and the text spells it with ס. Scripture
treats pronunciation as something that varied between regions, and records
it.

### The Greeks took the letters without their meanings

The Greeks borrowed the Phoenician alphabet around the 9th–8th century BC,
keeping its order and names: *alpha, beta, gamma, delta* are *ʾalp, bayt,
gaml, dalt*, meaningless in Greek. Some letters became vowels (aleph became
alpha, ayin became omicron). The letters passed from one language to another
as sounds.

### Related languages share roots in unrelated scripts

Akkadian (cuneiform syllables), Ugaritic (a cuneiform alphabet), Arabic and
Ge'ez share roots with Hebrew in scripts whose signs have nothing to do with
the Hebrew letters' pictures. A root's meaning travels with its sounds, in
whatever script records them.

## What this means for the lexicon

- **A word's meaning is shown from its use**, in scripture first, then from
  its cognates. The letters' pictures are not evidence of meaning.
- **Behind a merged letter there may be two older roots.** One Hebrew spelling
  can hold two words that were once spoken differently (ערב "evening", whose
  cognates have ġ; ערב "pledge", whose cognates have ʿ). Each sense records
  the cognates that show which sound it goes with, and `db lexicon-splits`
  collects these across the lexicon.
- **Hebrew and Aramaic forms are related through their sounds**: זָהָב and
  דְּהַב are one word from one older ḏ, recorded with the letter each dialect
  used for it.
- **Patterns among roots are to be found in the sounds.** Roots that seem to
  share two consonants (פרד, פרץ, פרק, פרר, פרש, all about separating) may form
  families, but only when grouped by their older sounds, not by Hebrew letters
  that hide merged sounds.
